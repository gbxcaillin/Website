# Positioning & strategy

Positioning is the decision that makes every other decision easier. It answers: for whom
are we the obvious choice, at what, and why us over the alternatives. Get this sharp and
the tagline, the design, and the campaigns almost fall out of it. Leave it fuzzy and no
amount of craft downstream will save the brand.

Produce five things. Fill `assets/brand-brief-template.md` as you go.

## 1. Target audience
Pick the *primary* audience that most moves the goal. Resist "everyone."
- Segment: who they are (role, firmographics/demographics, context).
- Jobs to be done: what they're really trying to achieve; the functional and emotional job.
- Pains and gains: what frustrates them today; what a win looks like.
- Where they are: where they look for solutions and who they trust.
- Decisive secondary audiences (referrers, partners, hires, regulators) noted separately —
  addressed with secondary messages, never by averaging the primary message into blandness.

## 2. Value proposition
Use a value-proposition statement, not a slogan:

> For **[target]** who **[need/situation]**, **[brand]** is the **[category]** that
> **[key benefit]**, because **[reason to believe / differentiator]**. Unlike
> **[main alternative]**, we **[what only we do]**.

The last two clauses do the work. If you can't complete "Unlike X, we…", you haven't
differentiated yet — go back to the competitive map.

## 3. Positioning statement (the one-liner)
Distil to a single sentence the whole company can repeat. It should be specific enough that
a competitor couldn't honestly say it too. Draft 3, pick 1, keep the runners-up.

## 4. Competitive frame & differentiation
- Restate the two-axis map from the audit; state the exact white space this brand will own.
- List 3–5 **differentiators** that are true, relevant to the buyer, and hard to copy. Rank
  them. Distinguish real differentiators from table stakes (things you must have but that
  don't win — don't build the brand on table stakes).
- **Proof points**: for each differentiator, the evidence that makes it believable (results,
  method, credentials, guarantees, named clients). Positioning without proof is a wish.

## 5. Brand strategy foundations
- **Brand idea / big idea**: the central organising thought (1 line) everything ladders to.
- **Personality**: 3–5 traits (e.g. precise, calm, commercially sharp) — chosen to
  differentiate, with the trait you'd defend against its opposite.
- **Values**: what the brand stands for and won't compromise (only if genuine).
- **Brand architecture** (if multiple products/lines): branded house (one master brand,
  e.g. Google Maps/Drive), house of brands (independent, e.g. P&G), or endorsed. State
  which and why, and how sub-brands relate to the master.
- **Messaging pillars**: 3–4 themes that support the positioning; each becomes a content
  and campaign territory later.

## Guardrails
- Position against a real alternative, including "do nothing" — often the true competitor.
- One primary message. Secondary audiences get tailored proof, not a different promise.
- In regulated sectors, the value proposition must not imply a status, licence, approval or
  guaranteed outcome the client can't substantiate. Flag anything close for compliance
  review, and prefer verifiable, specific claims over grand ones.
- Differentiation beats description. "We do X, Y, Z" is a list; positioning is a choice.

## Output format
```
# Positioning & strategy — [Client]

## Executive summary
## Primary audience (and secondary audiences)
## Value proposition
## Positioning statement (chosen + alternatives)
## Competitive frame & white space
## Differentiators + proof points (ranked)
## Brand idea, personality, values
## Brand architecture (if applicable)
## Messaging pillars
```
