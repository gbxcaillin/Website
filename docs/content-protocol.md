# Content protocol: education without financial product advice

How GBX Professional Services produces financial education, articles, tools,
workshops and training without providing financial product advice.

GBX PS Pty Ltd does not hold an Australian Financial Services Licence (AFSL).
Everything the firm publishes or delivers on financial topics must therefore
sit outside the definition of "financial product advice" in section 766B of
the Corporations Act 2001. This protocol is how we make sure it does. It is a
working rule set, not legal advice. Have a financial services lawyer review
it once in writing, and again if the offer changes.

Applies to: website articles and tools, newsletters, LinkedIn and other social
posts, workshops and webinars, courses, slide decks, handouts, client emails
that touch financial topics, and anything a team member says in a session.

## 1. The line we stay on the right side of

Financial product advice is a recommendation or a statement of opinion that
is intended to influence, or could reasonably be regarded as intended to
influence, a person's decision about a financial product or a class of
financial products. Intent is judged objectively: what a reasonable reader
would take from it, not what we meant.

Two things fall outside that definition and are what we produce:

1. **Factual information.** Objectively verifiable statements with no opinion
   attached. How compounding works. What a managed fund is. The current
   concessional contribution cap. How to read a product disclosure statement.
2. **Generic financial matters not about a financial product.** Budgeting,
   cash-flow habits, debt behaviour in principle, how markets work in
   principle, how to prepare for a meeting with an adviser, how to read a set
   of accounts.

A disclaimer does not turn advice into information. If a piece is advice, a
"general information only" line does not fix it. Change the content.

References: Corporations Act 2001 s766B; ASIC Regulatory Guide 244 (Giving
information, general advice and scaled advice); ASIC Regulatory Guide 36
(Licensing: Financial product advice and dealing).

## 2. The four-question test

Every piece of financial content passes all four before it goes out.

| # | Question | Pass |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Does it contain any opinion, preference or recommendation about a financial product or class of products (shares, ETFs, managed funds, super funds, insurance, loans, term deposits, crypto and so on)? | No |
| 2 | Could a reasonable reader come away thinking they should buy, sell, hold, switch, fix, top up or avoid something? | No |
| 3 | Was any personal information about the reader's circumstances, goals or finances collected or discussed in order to shape it? | No |
| 4 | Is every factual claim verifiable, current and sourced, and is every concept explained in principle rather than as a course of action? | Yes |

One failure means the piece is rewritten, not disclaimed.

## 3. Permitted and not permitted

Permitted, with examples in the firm's voice:

- Explaining a concept. "Diversification means spreading money across
  investments that do not move together, so one bad result does not decide
  the outcome."
- Describing how something works. "A franking credit represents tax the
  company has already paid on the profit behind a dividend."
- Describing what happened, in principle. "When interest rates rise, existing
  fixed-rate bonds tend to fall in price because newer bonds pay more."
- Explaining the questions a person could ask a licensed adviser.
- Explaining the mechanics of a process: how to read a statement, how a
  super contribution is made, what a product disclosure statement contains.
- Behavioural and literacy content: budgeting, saving habits, common
  decision biases, how to think about risk in principle.
- Business finance for business owners: margins, cash flow, unit economics,
  pricing, benchmarking. This is not financial product advice at all.

Not permitted, in any channel, with any disclaimer:

- "X is a good option", "most people should", "we prefer", "now is a good
  time to", "you are better off with", "consider switching to".
- Naming a product or provider alongside an opinion, comparison or ranking.
- Model portfolios, allocations, target weightings or "sample" portfolios.
- Market calls: where rates, markets, sectors or currencies are heading.
- Any content shaped by a reader's stated circumstances (see section 5).
- Opinions on specific securities or funds prepared for advisers or
  investment managers. That is a financial service to a wholesale client and
  still needs a licence. Frameworks, process and education only.
- Credit product steering (which loan, whether to fix, whether to refinance)
  and tax positions for a specific person. Stay conceptual on both.

## 4. Audiences and what changes

**Licensees, advisers and their staff (business to business).** Training on
compliance, ethics, advice process, technical product knowledge and soft
skills is a service to the firm, not a financial service to a client. It is
the freest space we operate in. The one limit: no research or opinions about
specific products, even for a wholesale audience.

**Business owners and leaders.** Business performance content (the bulk of
the site) does not touch financial products. The four-question test still
applies to anything that drifts into super, investing, insurance or lending.

**Consumers and the public.** Financial literacy only, factual and conceptual.
This is the audience where the line is easiest to cross. Every consumer-facing
piece gets the review in section 7.

**Corporate wellbeing sessions.** Treat the employees as consumers. No Q&A
that turns into personal advice (see section 5 for the script).

## 5. The personal-circumstances rule

We never collect, ask about or discuss an individual's personal financial
situation, goals or needs in order to shape what we tell them. Education
shaped to a person's circumstances looks like personal advice whatever it is
called.

In practice:

- Tools on the site ask about a business, never about a person's own money.
- Workshops take questions in principle. If a question is about the asker's
  own situation, use the script below.
- Enquiry forms and email capture do not ask for personal financial details.
- If someone volunteers their circumstances in an email, do not respond to
  the circumstances. Respond in principle and refer on.

Script for "what should I do?" questions, in a session or in writing:

> That is a question about your own situation, and answering it would be
> personal financial advice, which needs a licensed adviser. What I can do
> is explain how [the concept] works in general, and suggest the questions
> worth taking to an adviser.

## 6. Referrals

We may refer someone to a licensed adviser or firm. We pass on a name and
contact details only. We do not recommend a product, and we disclose any
benefit we receive for the referral at the time we make it. We do not receive
benefits tied to the person taking up a product.

## 7. Required wording

On every consumer-facing piece that touches investing, super, insurance,
lending or personal finance (articles, handouts, slides, emails, tools):

> This is general information and education only. It does not take account
> of your objectives, financial situation or needs and is not personal
> financial advice. GBX Professional Services does not hold an Australian
> Financial Services Licence. Consider speaking with a licensed adviser
> before acting on anything here.

Site footer (already in place): the firm provides business consulting,
analytics, education and training; does not hold an AFSL; does not provide
financial product advice, dealing or any other financial service.

Wording in this document is a floor. Never soften it; a lawyer may ask you
to lengthen it.

## 8. Review before publication

Every financial piece (consumer-facing or B2B) is reviewed by a second person
before it goes out. The reviewer works through this list and records the
outcome.

- [ ] Four-question test passed (section 2).
- [ ] No product or provider named alongside an opinion, comparison or ranking.
- [ ] No "should", "consider", "better off", "good option" or similar aimed at a product decision.
- [ ] No market call.
- [ ] No content shaped by a reader's circumstances; no personal data collected.
- [ ] Facts checked against a primary source (legislation, ASIC, ATO, the product's own PDS) and dated where they can change.
- [ ] Required wording present (section 7) where the topic calls for it.
- [ ] Firm name written in full; no em dashes; house style followed.
- [ ] Reviewer name and date recorded.

Record: keep a simple log (date, title, channel, author, reviewer, outcome).
A spreadsheet is enough. It is the evidence that the protocol is real.

## 9. Live sessions and social media

- Prepare slides and talking points to this protocol. Do not improvise on
  products.
- In Q&A, use the section 5 script for anything personal.
- On LinkedIn and in newsletters, the same rules apply to a two-line post as
  to a long article. Replies and comments count.
- Do not repost or endorse third-party content that contains product
  opinions. Sharing it with approval can be treated as making the statement.

## 10. If we get it wrong

If a piece is found to cross the line, take it down or correct it the same
day, note it in the review log, and tell the team what changed. If it went to
an audience (a newsletter, a session), send a short correction. Do not argue
the point in public.

## 11. Growing beyond education

If the firm ever wants to give general advice (opinions about product
classes, market views with a call to action), the options are to become an
authorised representative or corporate authorised representative of an AFSL
holder, or to apply for a licence. Neither is quick. Until one is in place,
this protocol is the boundary.
