Part one

Pricing the education practice

Three ways to structure the fees, what they cost to deliver, what the market charges, and a recommended menu you can put in front of the first ten prospects. Every figure is a proposal for you to set; nothing is published on the site until you say so.

1. What we are pricing against

Your cost to deliver. The build is a one-off: roughly eight hours per module to write and review to the content protocol, so about 48 hours for the six. After that, each session is 1 to 1.5 hours of delivery, about an hour of scheduling and travel, and half an hour of follow-up. Call it three hours of senior time per delivered session once the build is done.

The market. Indicative ranges from general knowledge of the Australian workplace wellbeing market; verify with two quotes before finalising. Standalone financial wellbeing sessions from specialist providers commonly sit between $1,500 and $4,000 per session. Multi-session programs run $8,000 to $30,000 depending on headcount and customisation. Subscription platforms with recorded webinars charge $30 to $120 per employee per year. Keynote speakers on money topics charge $3,000 to $8,000 for a single hour.

The value anchor. Employers already buy wellbeing. The comparison that lands is per head: a program for 60 staff at $12,500 is about $208 a person, less than the average cost of a single day of absence once wages and lost output are counted. Say that number in every sales conversation.

5. Terms to set alongside the fees

Commercial terms

  • 50% on booking, balance on completion for programs; sessions invoiced on delivery.
  • Rescheduling free with 10 business days' notice; 50% inside that; 100% inside 2 days.
  • Headcount confirmed at booking; per-head add-on reconciled at the end.
  • Fees reviewed annually, not mid-program.

Compliance terms

  • Every session opens with the education-not-advice statement.
  • No personal financial information collected from participants; surveys ask about the session only.
  • Practice seminars name the practice as the party for personal advice.
  • Materials reviewed to the content protocol and logged before delivery.

6. Decisions for you

  1. Accept, adjust or replace the menu figures. My recommendation is to start exactly here and lift the program fee by $1,500 once two case studies exist.
  2. Publish the fees on the Education page, or keep "Quoted per program". Publishing the lunch-and-learn and program fees is the stronger move: it qualifies enquiries and matches the "fixed fee, agreed up front" positioning across the site.
  3. Run the launch pricing for ninety days, or not at all.
  4. Offer the recording licence from day one, or hold it until the modules have been delivered live a few times.
  5. Confirm the payment and cancellation terms above.

When decided: set education.pricing in src/content.js and record the figures in docs/go-to-market.md. The Education page is already built to show them.

Why we teach

A confession from someone with an education degree and a financial planning qualification.

Most financial education is not designed to be understood. It is designed to be compliant, or to be sold from. Those are different goals, and the person in the room can tell.

Teaching money well is the same as teaching anything well. Start with what the learner already knows. Use one clear example, not six. Say what a thing is before you say why it matters. Leave time for the questions, because the questions are the lesson.

That is the whole method behind the Financial Fluency Program. Nothing clever. Just teaching, applied to a subject that badly needs it.

One rule we hold ourselves to: we explain how things work and the questions worth asking. We never tell anyone what to buy, sell or switch. That is a licensed adviser's job, and we say so in every session.

Explainer

Volatility is not the same as loss. It is the single most useful distinction in financial literacy, and almost nobody is taught it.

Volatility is movement. A value goes up and down along the way. It feels like risk, especially when the news is loud, but a thing can move around a great deal and still be there at the end.

Permanent loss is capital you do not get back. A business that fails. A holding that never recovers. Or, most commonly, selling in a panic and turning a temporary dip into a real one.

Which risk matters most depends on when you need the money. Money for next year and money for thirty years from now face different risks, and treating them the same is where a lot of stress comes from.

That is the whole lesson. We teach it in about eight minutes, with one example.

Explainer

Compounding, without the hype.

When returns start earning returns, growth builds on growth. In the early years it looks unremarkable, which is why people give up on it. The impressive part comes later, because each period builds on a bigger base than the one before.

Two things people miss.

It rewards time more than timing. Starting earlier, with less, often beats starting later with more.

And it works in both directions. Fees compound against you over the years. High-interest debt compounds against you just as relentlessly as returns compound for you.

Understanding compounding is as much about respecting the downside as admiring the upside. That is the version we teach.

General information and education only, not personal financial advice.

Positioning

We did not call it financial literacy. We called it fluency. Here is why.

Literacy sounds like remediation. Something you lack. A box to tick.

Fluency is what you have when you can use a language without thinking about it. Ask a question in it. Follow a conversation. Notice when something does not add up.

That is what we want for the people in our programs. Not a certificate. Confidence. The ability to read a statement, sit in a meeting with an adviser and ask the right thing, and not feel like an outsider to their own money.

Two of our leaders came to this from teaching and languages. It shows in the name, and it shows in the room.
